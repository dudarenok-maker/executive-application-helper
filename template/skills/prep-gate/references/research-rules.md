# Research depth and sourcing rules — Check 5

The reference for `prep-gate` Check 5. The authoring-side rules live in
`candidate-voice/references/interview-prep-template.md` §R and §§3–6; this file is the verification
side. Everything here exists because sections 3–6 are the only part of a prep pack that asserts
things about **other people's companies and other people's careers** — the half that can embarrass
the candidate in the room rather than merely thin the pack.

---

## 1. The confidence ladder — the vocabulary a claim must carry

| Status | Phrasing that satisfies the row | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| First-party confirmed | "confirmed via their own leadership page", "confirmed via the organisation's own current people page (updated [date])" | The strongest marker. Name the page, not just "their website" |
| Independently corroborated | "confirmed via the company's own leadership page and independently corroborated via [named publication]" | Two independent sources, one first-party |
| Reasonably corroborated | "confirmed and reasonably corroborated" | Multiple secondary sources; no first-party page |
| Secondary only | "reported via professional-network sources, not confirmed on their own site" | One aggregator or professional-network profile only. Must say so |
| Unconfirmed / refused | "The specific field of the doctorate is not confirmed … don't guess at or repeat a specific field" | The gap is named **and** the guess is forbidden |
| Negative finding | "No independently confirmed public career history could be located for her" | A legitimate, usable entry — better than a plausible guess |
| Inference | bolded `(inference)` lead-in on its own paragraph | Never inside a confirmed-fact paragraph |

A claim carrying none of these is unmarked. Unmarked **person**-claims fail row 5.6 and block;
unmarked figure, certification or system claims fail row 5.5 and are advisory.

## 2. Source-authority ranking, and conflict resolution

1. The organisation's own **current** page
2. Dated direct intel from the candidate, the interview invitation, or the calendar event
3. A reputable named publication
4. A professional-network profile
5. A data aggregator

Where sources conflict, the document must **name the conflict**, name which source it treats as
authoritative, and say why. Two illustrative cases:

- **Superseded first-party name.** A secondary source names one person as CEO; the company's own
  current About Us page names someone else. The current first-party page wins, and the superseded
  name is flagged as a likely predecessor rather than silently dropped.
- **Stale first-party page vs a fresher direct source.** An organisation's own senior-officers page
  gives a panellist a different portfolio from the one in the interview invitation. The invitation
  wins as the more recent and more direct source, and the discrepancy is written into a **Research
  caveat** paragraph at the foot of Sources.

An unresolved conflict left unnamed in the document fails row 5.5.

## 3. Inference-labelling rule (row 5.4 — blocking)

Speculative language is easy to grep and easy to lose in a reformat. Search the research sections for
`likely`, `expect him`, `expect her`, `strongly suggests`, `reads as`, `probably`, `almost certainly`,
`it's reasonable to expect`. Every hit must sit inside a paragraph whose bolded lead-in carries
`(inference)` — canonically **Likely voice and tone (inference).** or **Likely working style
(inference from career shape).** — and §5 must open with the sourcing-disclaimer blockquote naming
what the inference is built from.

One exception worth recognising rather than failing: `almost certainly` inside the
structural-reality section ("what each panellist is almost certainly testing") is analysis of the
round, not a claim about a person's biography. Note it in the evidence cell and pass the row.

## 4. Person-claim sourcing rule (row 5.6 — blocking)

For each named individual in the document, list every claim made about them and the source phrase
attached to it. A claim with no source phrase in the same sentence fails the row. This includes
claims that flatter — a job title, a tenure, a qualification — and claims that read as harmless.
The failure mode this catches is the one that ends a conversation: the candidate repeats a fact
about a panellist in the room, the panellist corrects them, and the rest of the pack loses its
authority.

## 5. Handling markers (advisory, row 5.5)

Some facts are worth knowing and wrong to raise. The document must mark them at the point of use:

- `Background awareness only — not a topic to raise unprompted` — board or leadership changes
- `a personal detail worth knowing rather than using as a talking point` — family, hobbies
- `hold in reserve` — technical detail that only helps if the panel goes there first

## 6. Cross-round hygiene (row 5.9 — blocking, multi-round packs only)

Verified by diffing the earlier round's Sources — particularly its dated direct-intel entries —
against the later brief's and addendum's text. The later document must carry:

- an explicit directive naming what must not be referenced and why
  (*"Do not reference [X]'s personal situation in this room. That context was shared with you in
  confidence and relates to Round 1 specifically"*),
- the same boundary repeated as a one-line **Remember:** anchor in the addendum, and
- a pre-scripted clean, generic answer to "what did you discuss last round?"

## 7. Domain-term explainers (advisory, row 5.8)

A term belonging to the target's world rather than the candidate's needs three things: what the
standard or term is, **which part of it actually matters here**, and what it means in practice on the
ground. The test is whether the candidate could use the term correctly under a follow-up question,
not whether they could say it.

*Illustration.* A supply-chain employer's ads and systems talk about a global identifier standard.
A usable explainer does not recite the whole standard: it says what the body is, discards the
consumer-facing meaning the candidate might reach for by reflex, keeps the one identifier type that
actually governs this employer's operations, and then says what that means on the ground — that a
consignment can be scanned consistently across depots, partner carriers and customer docks with no
proprietary lock-in and no manual re-labelling at handover. Three sentences, and the term is now
usable under a follow-up rather than merely pronounceable.

## 8. Conditional sections — skipped is fine, silently absent is not

§4 (entities) and §6 (structural-reality analysis) are conditional. Where the trigger does not
apply, the document should **say it was considered and skipped**. A row marked `N-A` must carry that
reason in the evidence cell; `N-A` asserted without a reason is `UNVERIFIED`, and `UNVERIFIED` blocks.
